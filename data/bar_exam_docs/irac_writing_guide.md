# IRAC Essay Writing Guide – California Bar Exam

## What is IRAC?

IRAC stands for Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion. It is the fundamental framework for legal analysis on the California Bar Exam.

## Structure

### Basic IRAC
```
ISSUE:    State the legal question raised by the facts
RULE:     State the governing legal rule/doctrine
APPLICATION: Apply the rule to the specific facts
CONCLUSION:  State your conclusion
```

### Nested IRAC: IR(irac)C

When a rule contains sub-parts, elements, or exceptions, each one gets its own mini-IRAC within the Rule section:

```
ISSUE 1: [Main issue]
RULE: [Main rule with elements]
    SUB-ISSUE 1a: [First element/sub-issue]
    RULE: [Sub-rule for this element]
    APPLICATION: [Apply to facts]
    CONCLUSION: [Mini-conclusion]
    
    SUB-ISSUE 1b: [Second element/sub-issue]
    RULE: [Sub-rule]
    APPLICATION: [Apply to facts] 
    CONCLUSION: [Mini-conclusion]
APPLICATION: [Overall application tying sub-analyses together]
CONCLUSION: [Final conclusion on the main issue]
```

## Key Principles for High-Scoring Essays

### Issue Spotting
- Read the call of the question first
- Identify ALL issues – missing issues = lost points
- Major issues deserve full IRAC; minor issues can be brief
- Signal transitions: "The next issue is whether..."

### Rule Statements
- State rules precisely and completely
- Include elements, definitions, and standards
- Cite California-specific rules when they differ from common law
- Use "Under California law..." or "The majority rule..." framing

### Application
- THIS IS WHERE MOST POINTS ARE EARNED
- Apply EACH element to SPECIFIC facts from the prompt
- Argue BOTH sides: "Plaintiff will argue... Defendant will counter..."
- Don't just recite rules – show HOW they apply
- Use the facts! Quote or reference specific facts given

### Conclusion
- Be definitive: "Therefore, the court will likely find..."
- Brief acknowledgment of strongest counterargument
- Tie to the overall question asked

## Time Management (typical 1-hour essay)
- Read & outline: 10 minutes
- Write: 45 minutes
- Review: 5 minutes

## Example: Nested IRAC for Negligence

**ISSUE: Is Defendant liable to Plaintiff for negligence?**

**RULE:** Negligence requires proof of four elements: (1) duty, (2) breach, (3) causation, and (4) damages.

  **SUB-ISSUE: Duty**
  **RULE:** Under the Rowland v. Christian factors, California imposes a general duty of care on all persons to act as a reasonably prudent person under the circumstances.
  **APPLICATION:** Here, Defendant was operating a vehicle on a public road. All drivers owe a duty of care to other users of the road, including Plaintiff who was a pedestrian in a marked crosswalk. Therefore, Defendant owed Plaintiff a duty of care.
  **CONCLUSION:** Duty is established.

  **SUB-ISSUE: Breach**
  **RULE:** Breach occurs when the defendant's conduct falls below the standard of care. Under the Learned Hand formula, breach exists when the burden of precaution (B) is less than the probability of harm (P) multiplied by the gravity of the resulting injury (L).
  **APPLICATION:** Defendant was texting while driving at 45 mph in a 25 mph school zone. The burden of not texting while driving is minimal, while the probability and severity of harm from distracted speeding in a school zone are significant. A reasonably prudent driver would not text while driving, especially in a school zone. Defendant's conduct clearly falls below this standard.
  **CONCLUSION:** Defendant breached the duty of care.

  **SUB-ISSUE: Causation**
  **RULE:** Causation has two components: actual cause (but-for test) and proximate cause (foreseeability).
  **APPLICATION:** But for Defendant's distracted driving and speeding, Defendant would have seen Plaintiff in the crosswalk and stopped in time. The harm was a foreseeable result of texting while speeding through a pedestrian area. No superseding cause intervened.
  **CONCLUSION:** Both actual and proximate causation are satisfied.

  **SUB-ISSUE: Damages**
  **RULE:** Plaintiff must prove actual damages resulting from the defendant's breach.
  **APPLICATION:** Plaintiff suffered a broken leg, $50,000 in medical bills, lost wages, and pain and suffering — all actual, quantifiable damages directly resulting from the collision.
  **CONCLUSION:** Damages are established.

**APPLICATION (Overall):** Applying all four elements, Plaintiff has strong claims on each. The strongest defense would be comparative negligence if Plaintiff was distracted, but the facts don't indicate this.

**CONCLUSION:** Defendant is likely liable to Plaintiff for negligence. Under California's pure comparative negligence system, any negligence by Plaintiff would reduce but not bar recovery.
